What are we to make of \emph{Sirāt} (Óliver Laxe, 2025), other than as
one of the bleakest depictions in recent cinematic history of the brutal
randomness of the human condition? Or as further evidence in support of
Bruno Latour's contention that ``we have never been modern''?

Shot mostly in the crushing heat of the Moroccan desert in the summer of
2024, the Spanish-French co-production was awarded the Jury Prize at the
Cannes Film Festival 2025, in spite (or perhaps because) of its
emotionally devastating and graphic content. This paper attempts to make
sense of the film by framing it in relation to two historical
antecedents: the long history of cinematic representations of the desert
in Hollywood and other world cinemas; and the countercultural imaginary
of Morocco as a site of societal and sexual freedom, from the writings
of Paul Bowles and Jean Genet, or Brian Jones's musical encounter with
the Jajouka brotherhood, to the colonisation of the Moroccan desert
(amongst others) by the ecstatic nomads of rave culture.

While as the contributors to a recent collection of essays and creative
writing pointedly titled \emph{Deserts Are Not Empty} (Samia Henni, ed.,
2022) argue, western cinema has typically depicted deserts within a
colonial worldview, as empty spaces awaiting territorialization by
imperial powers, the ravers of \emph{Sirāt} soon discover that the
apparently empty territory they occupy is traversed by local
geopolitical struggles and the larger transnational forces that shape
them. Even the supposedly pristine wilderness of the desert is striated
by the minefield that ultimately obliterates most of the cast still left
standing. Rave culture itself, while ostensibly a hedonistic alternative
practice to western bourgeois society, is revealed as a fantasy of
escape from the geopolitical landscapes that they seek to claim and
inhabit like colonial settlers, and as just another form of alienation
from the brutal realities of the world.

While \emph{Sirāt}'s vision of the world may seem unremittingly bleak,
the paper argues, the film can also be read as a scathing indictment of
human alienation both from the planet itself and of the societies that
continue to contest its sublime landscapes.

\subsection{Bio}\label{bio}

Martin Roberts is a faculty member in the School of Film, Television,
and Media Arts at Emerson College. His research focuses on transnational
media cultures. His publications include essays on ethnographic
documentary, the globalisation of IMAX cinema, the concept of film
culture, the Dogma `95 movement, postfeminist television, and global
subcultures. His most recent book is on Japanese popular music for the
33 1/3 album series, titled \emph{Cornelius's 'Fantasma'}. His current
research focuses on the concept of the 'cinematic' and its legacy in
contemporary digital culture.
